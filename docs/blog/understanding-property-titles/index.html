C of O, Governor's Consent & Deed of Assignment: A Buyer's Guide to Property Titles in Nigeria

When you start shopping for property anywhere in Nigeria, you'll hear the same phrases over and over: Certificate of Occupancy, Governor's Consent, and Deed of Assignment. Sellers throw them around like badges of honour — and some hope you won't ask what they actually mean. Let's fix that. In plain language, here is what each document is, and how to protect yourself — wherever in Nigeria you're buying.

The Land Use Act: the one law behind every title

Every property document in Nigeria flows from a single law: the Land Use Act of 1978. It vests ownership of all land in each state in the state governor (in the Federal Capital Territory, the Minister of the FCT), who in turn grants rights of occupancy to individuals and organisations. Once you understand this, the paperwork starts making sense.

Certificate of Occupancy (C of O)

The strongest title you can hold anywhere in Nigeria. A C of O is a statutory right of occupancy granted by the state governor and registered at that state's land registry. Banks, courts and future buyers across the country treat it as the gold standard. Our Key Haven Estate in Kajola, Ogun State, for example, carries a C of O — which is one reason it commands a premium.

Governor's Consent

Section 22 of the Land Use Act requires the governor's consent for any transfer of a statutory right of occupancy — in every state. So when you buy property whose seller holds a C of O, you perfect your own ownership by obtaining Governor's Consent on the transaction. (In the FCT, the equivalent is the Minister's consent.) Our Okun Ajah duplex and the Ikate penthouse in Lagos both carry Governor's Consent.

Deed of Assignment & Registered Survey

From Epe to Ikorodu to the Sagamu axis and beyond, most estate land in Nigeria is sold on a Deed of Assignment — the legal instrument transferring the seller's interest to you — supported by a Registered Survey lodged at the state surveyor-general's office, which maps your plot's exact coordinates. This is a perfectly legitimate way to own land in any state, provided the underlying title is clean. Our Maven and Key Haven estates are sold on Deed of Assignment & Registered Survey.

Customary Rights & Government Allocation

In rural and peri-urban communities, land may be held under customary rights granted by families, communities or local governments. These are valid — but confirm them with the actual family/community heads and check the land was properly excised from any government acquisition. Government Allocation (federal or state) means the land was allocated directly by government, usually with an allocation letter and often progressing toward a C of O. Our Key Haven Phase 1 in Ilara sits on Government Allocation.

Titles differ by state — so verify locally

Each state runs its own land registry and surveyor-general's office, and document names and procedures vary slightly from state to state. The principle never changes though: verify at the registry of the state where the land sits, not where you live.

The one question to ask before you pay

"Can I verify this document at the land registry / surveyor-general's office before I pay?" A genuine seller will say yes and facilitate the search — in Lagos, Ogun, or anywhere else.
