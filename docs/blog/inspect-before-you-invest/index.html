Inspect Before You Invest: How to Physically Check a Property Before You Pay

The photos looked perfect. The video tour was flawless. Then you visited — and the "serene neighbourhood" was a traffic-choked junction, the "newly built" walls were hiding damp, and the land was waterlogged. Physical inspection is the step that separates confident buyers from regretful ones. Here's how to do it like a professional.

For completed homes: the room-by-room walkthrough

Visit twice — at different times of day. Morning and evening visits reveal different truths: traffic patterns, noise levels, security presence, and — crucially in our climate — how the area behaves after heavy rain. A street that is dry in December can be a river in July.

Check walls, ceilings and floors. Distinguish hairline plaster cracks (cosmetic) from deep structural cracks (danger). Look for water stains on ceilings — they betray roof leaks. Walk the floors: tiles should sound solid, not hollow.

Test the plumbing. Run every tap, flush every toilet, check water pressure on the top floor, and watch how fast sinks drain. Ask the source: borehole? Treated water? How reliable is supply?

Test the electricals. Flip every switch, try every socket. Then ask the bigger question: what is the power arrangement? Which PHCN band or feeder serves the area, is there a dedicated transformer, and what backup exists — inverter, solar, generator? In estates, ask how power is managed.

Check doors, windows and locks. Every key should be handed over; burglary-proofing should be intact and functional.

Match the building to its approval. The approved building plan should match what stands on the ground — our Ikate penthouse, for example, carries Building Approval. Unapproved extensions can become government demolition notices.

Study the neighbourhood. Is the road tarred and motorable in the rainy season? How is estate security and management run? Who are the neighbours? How far is work, school, worship?

For land: walk it, don't just view it

Walk the exact plot with a surveyor — confirm the beacons and that the coordinates match the survey plan. Never accept "it's around here."

Study the topography. Waterlogged or very low-lying land costs far more to develop — think sand-filling and raft foundations. If possible, visit during or just after rain.

Confirm the access road. Is there a real, motorable road to the plot today — or only a promise on a brochure?

Look at what is rising around it. Neighbouring developments signal genuine growth and confirm the axis is actually developing, not just marketed.

Confirm the plot's identity. The classic swap scam: you're shown plot A and sold plot B. A surveyor with the coordinates on the survey plan kills this fraud instantly.

Red flags — pause when you see these

A seller who discourages physical inspection or rushes you with "someone else is paying tomorrow." No survey plan or surveyor made available. A building that differs from its approved plan. Visible structural cracks, persistent damp, or suspiciously fresh paint over everything. Land with no clear access road.
