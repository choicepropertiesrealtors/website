What If the Land You Paid For Has Already Been Allocated to Someone Else? How to Avoid Multiple Allocation

You paid. You got a receipt. Months later you visit your plot and someone is already building on it, holding papers that say the land is theirs. This is called multiple allocation: one plot sold or allocated to more than one buyer. It happens in Lagos, Ogun, Abuja and across Nigeria. Here is how it happens, and how to protect yourself.

How multiple allocation happens

Deliberate fraud: a seller collects money from several buyers for the same plot and disappears, or keeps delaying.

Family disputes: different branches of a landowning family sell the same land to different people, each claiming the right to sell.

Poor record-keeping: a developer or seller keeps sloppy records, and plot numbers get reused or mixed up.

Re-selling after default: a buyer stops paying, the seller resells the plot, then the first buyer comes back with old receipts.

No physical allocation: buyers pay but never go to the site to be shown their exact plot with beacons, so nobody can tell where one plot ends and another begins.

Warning signs before you pay

The seller will not show you the exact plot, only "the general area".

There is no survey plan with your plot's own coordinates and beacon numbers.

The seller asks for cash or payment into a personal account.

The price is far below everyone else's in the same area.

The seller pushes you to "pay today before it is gone".

How to protect yourself

  1. Verify the title first. Find out what backs the land: C of O, Governor's Consent, Government Allocation, or registered survey with deed of assignment. Confirm it at the state Lands Registry or Surveyor-General's office. (See our guide to understanding property titles.)
  2. Get physical allocation. Go to the site and have the seller's surveyor show you your plot, with beacons in the ground. Take dated photos and a video of the beacons.
  3. Get a survey plan in your name showing your plot's coordinates and beacon numbers. It is your strongest proof of exactly which land is yours.
  4. Get an allocation letter that names the plot number, size and estate, signed by the seller.
  5. Pay into a company account only, and keep every receipt in your own name.
  6. Fence or mark the plot early. A fenced plot with your name on a small sign is much harder to resell. Visit regularly.
  7. Buy from a seller with a track record. An estate that already has buyers building, roads and an office is far less likely to double-sell.

What to do if it has happened to you

Do not fight on the land. Gather your documents: receipts, allocation letter, survey plan, photos.

Go back to the seller in writing and ask for the plot, or an equal plot, or a refund.

Get a lawyer. Many cases are settled once the seller sees you have a lawyer and proof.

Report fraud to the police if the seller has collected money from several people on purpose.

The buyer with the earlier valid documents and physical possession usually stands stronger, which is why early allocation, a survey and fencing matter so much.

The bottom line

Multiple allocation is avoidable. Verify the title, get shown your exact plot with beacons, get papers in your own name, pay only into company accounts, and secure the plot early.
